📖 Experimental Manual: Spherometer & Optical Bench

1. Aim & Learning Objectives

To determine the radius of curvature ($R$) of a given spherical surface (convex/concave mirror or watch glass) using a spherometer, and to verify the optical relationship $R = 2f$ (i.e. $f = R/2$) using a concave mirror mounted on an optical bench via the no-parallax coincidence method.

2. Mathematical Derivation of Spherometer Formula

Let the three outer pointed legs of the spherometer form an equilateral triangle $ABC$ of side $l$. The radius of the circumscribing circle of $\triangle ABC$ is:

$$r = \frac{l}{\sqrt{3}}$$

Let $R$ be the radius of curvature of the spherical surface and $h$ be the sagitta (elevation or depression of the central screw tip relative to the plane of the three outer legs). By the geometric chord theorem for a circle of radius $R$:

$$(2R - h) \cdot h = r^2 = \left(\frac{l}{\sqrt{3}}\right)^2 = \frac{l^2}{3}$$ $$2Rh - h^2 = \frac{l^2}{3} \implies 2Rh = \frac{l^2}{3} + h^2$$ $$R = \frac{l^2}{6h} + \frac{h}{2}$$

3. Geometrical Optics & Center of Curvature Method

For a concave spherical mirror with radius of curvature $R$ and focal length $f = R/2$, the mirror equation gives:

$$\frac{1}{v} + \frac{1}{u} = \frac{1}{f} = \frac{2}{R}$$

When an upright illuminated object pin is placed at distance $u = R$ (at the Center of Curvature $C$):

$$\frac{1}{v} + \frac{1}{R} = \frac{2}{R} \implies \frac{1}{v} = \frac{1}{R} \implies v = R$$ $$\text{Magnification } m = -\frac{v}{u} = -\frac{R}{R} = -1$$

Thus, a real, inverted image of the same size is formed exactly at the tip of the object pin. When the observer moves their eye laterally, no parallax exists between the object needle tip and the real inverted image needle tip ($x_{\text{pin}} = x_{\text{image}}$).

4. Principle of Parallax Removal

When two objects are located at different distances from an observer's eye, moving the eye sideways causes the farther object to appear to move in the same direction as the eye, while the nearer object appears to move in the opposite direction. When both objects are coincident at the exact same spatial plane ($u = v = R$), relative apparent angular displacement vanishes ($\delta = 0$).

5. Viva Voce & Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: Why is the instrument named a 'Spherometer'?
Ans: Because it is primarily designed to measure the radius of curvature of spherical surfaces such as lenses, mirrors, and watch glasses.
Q2: What is the sagitta ($h$) of a spherical surface?
Ans: The sagitta is the perpendicular distance from the center of the arc of a circle to the center of its chord. In spherometer metrology, it is the vertical distance between the tip of the central screw and the plane passing through the three outer legs.
Q3: Why is the term $h/2$ added in the formula $R = \frac{l^2}{6h} + \frac{h}{2}$?
Ans: It arises from the chord theorem $(2R - h)h = r^2$. For large radii ($R \gg h$), $h/2$ is small but provides essential higher-order correction for high-precision metrology.
Q4: How do you detect whether the central screw touches the surface?
Ans: By the paper strip resistance test: a thin strip of paper placed under the central screw offers slight friction when pulled, without tilting the instrument on its three outer legs.
