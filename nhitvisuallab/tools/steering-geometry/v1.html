1. Ackermann Principle

Ensures inner front wheel turns at a larger angle than outer front wheel during cornering so all wheels roll about a single instantaneous center.

2. Camber Angle

Outward (+) or inward (-) tilt of the wheel top relative to true vertical. Improves tire contact patch during cornering.

3. Caster Angle

Rearward (+) tilt of the steering axis line. Provides directional stability and self-centering steering wheel return force.

4. Kingpin Inclination (KPI)

Inward inclination of the steering swivel axis. Reduces scrub radius and steering effort.

📖 User Guide — Steering Geometry & Wheel Alignment Simulator
1 Overview

The Steering Geometry & Wheel Alignment Simulator visualises the five fundamental alignment angles: Camber, Caster, Toe, Kingpin Inclination (KPI), and Scrub Radius. Together these angles define how the wheel is positioned relative to the road and the steering axis, directly affecting straight-line stability, cornering ability, tyre wear, and steering effort.

Mechanics, vehicle dynamics students, and automotive engineers use this tool to understand how misalignment causes pull, shimmy, and uneven tyre wear, and how each angle should be corrected.

2 Adjusting Alignment Angles

Use the sliders to set each angle:

  • Camber (−5° to +5°) — inward (negative) or outward (positive) tilt of the wheel. Zero camber = vertical. Negative camber improves cornering grip; excessive negative camber wears inner tyre edge.
  • Caster (0° to 10°) — forward or rearward tilt of the steering axis. Positive caster gives straight-line stability and self-centering; too much caster increases steering effort.
  • Toe (−10mm to +10mm) — parallel alignment of the front wheels viewed from above. Toe-in = front edges closer together (stability). Toe-out = front edges apart (agility). Incorrect toe causes feathered tyre wear.
  • KPI (0° to 15°) — inward tilt of the steering axis (kingpin). Creates a virtual pivot point below the road surface, reducing scrub radius.
  • Scrub Radius (mm) — horizontal distance between the tyre contact centre and the steering axis ground contact point. Negative scrub improves braking stability.
3 Reading the Alignment Diagram

The canvas shows a front-view and top-view representation of the wheel. Coloured angle indicators show the current setting of each alignment parameter. The status panel on the right displays whether each angle is within specification (PASS / WARN / FAIL) based on typical passenger car tolerances.

4 Common Alignment Problems

Understanding how misalignment manifests as a symptom helps with diagnosis:

  • Vehicle pulls to one side → unequal camber or caster side-to-side.
  • Steering wheel vibration at highway speed → wheel imbalance or loose steering components rather than alignment alone.
  • Inner tyre wear → excessive negative camber.
  • Feathered tread blocks → incorrect toe setting.
  • Heavy steering → excessive positive caster or KPI.

Steering Geometry and Wheel Alignment Explained

Steering Geometry (also called wheel alignment) refers to the angles of the wheels relative to the vehicle and the road surface. Correct alignment ensures straight-line stability, predictable handling, even tyre wear, and accurate steering response. Misalignment causes pull, wander, premature tyre wear, and in severe cases, unsafe vehicle behaviour.

Camber Angle

Camber is the inward or outward tilt of the wheel when viewed from the front. Positive camber means the top of the wheel leans outward; negative camber means it leans inward. Modern vehicles typically use slight negative camber (−0.5° to −1.5°) to improve cornering grip because the tyre leans slightly into the corner under load. Excessive negative camber accelerates inner tyre wear.

Caster Angle

Caster is the forward or rearward tilt of the steering axis (the line through the upper and lower ball joints) when viewed from the side. Positive caster (steering axis tilts rearward at the top) creates a self-centering effect: the tyre contact patch trails behind the steering axis pivot, making the wheel want to return to straight-ahead after a turn. Typical positive caster: 3°–7° for passenger cars. Sports cars use more caster for sharper feel.

Toe Setting

Toe is the parallel alignment of the wheels viewed from above. Toe-in (front edges of tyres closer together) is used on rear-wheel-drive vehicles to compensate for the tendency of driven wheels to toe-out under load. Toe-out (front edges further apart) may be used on front-wheel-drive vehicles. Incorrect toe causes rapid tyre scrubbing — the most common tyre wear complaint traced to alignment.

Kingpin Inclination and Scrub Radius

Kingpin Inclination (KPI) is the inward tilt of the steering axis in the front view. Combined with camber angle, it determines the Included Angle = Camber + KPI. KPI moves the steering axis contact point on the road surface toward or beyond the tyre centreline. A negative scrub radius (axis contact point outside the tyre contact patch) improves braking stability and self-correction during a blowout by creating a toe-in moment when one brake is more effective than the other.
