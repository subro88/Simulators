📖 User Guide — Automotive Friction Clutch Simulator
1 Overview

The Automotive Friction Clutch Simulator models the behaviour of Single-Plate and Multi-Plate automotive friction clutches. It calculates torque capacity using the formula T = n × μ × W × Rm, shows friction wear, clutch slip, and pedal engagement force, and animates the pressure plate clamping action.

A clutch is a mechanical device that connects and disconnects the engine from the gearbox. During gear changes, the driver presses the clutch pedal, which releases the spring clamping force, allowing the engine and gearbox to momentarily spin independently.

2 Selecting Clutch Type

Use the Clutch Type buttons to switch between:

  • Single-Plate — one friction disc; standard in passenger cars. Simple, light, and sufficient for moderate torque.
  • Multi-Plate — multiple interleaved friction discs; used in motorcycles, high-performance cars, and trucks where higher torque capacity is needed in a compact package.
  • Diaphragm Spring — replaces the coil spring with a conical diaphragm spring for lower pedal effort and better clamp load consistency. Most modern cars use this type.
3 Adjusting Parameters

Five sliders control the clutch design parameters:

  • Clamp Force W (N) — the spring clamping force pressing the friction disc against the flywheel.
  • Friction Coefficient μ — depends on lining material (dry: 0.25–0.45; wet: 0.08–0.15).
  • Number of Plates n — 1 for single-plate; 2–8 for multi-plate.
  • Mean Radius Rm (mm) — average of inner and outer friction disc radius.
  • Engine RPM — input speed to calculate slip torque and engagement behaviour.
4 Reading the Readout

The live stats panel shows:

  • Torque Capacity T — maximum torque the clutch can transmit before slipping.
  • Slip Condition — whether the clutch is engaged, slipping, or disengaged.
  • Wear Rate — relative wear index based on pressure and velocity.
  • Pedal Force — estimated force the driver must apply to disengage the clutch.

How Automotive Friction Clutches Work

An Automotive Friction Clutch is the mechanical coupling between the engine flywheel and the gearbox input shaft. It allows smooth engagement and disengagement of the drive, enabling gear changes and vehicle starting from rest without stalling the engine.

Torque Capacity Formula

The torque capacity of a friction clutch is given by:

T = n × μ × W × Rm

Where: n = number of friction surfaces, μ = coefficient of friction, W = axial clamping force (N), and Rm = mean friction radius (m). Increasing any of these factors increases torque capacity. Multi-plate clutches multiply torque capacity by increasing n without increasing diameter.

Single-Plate vs Multi-Plate Clutches

A single-plate clutch has one friction disc sandwiched between the flywheel face and the pressure plate. It is simple, light, and easy to service — the standard choice for passenger cars. A multi-plate clutch uses multiple interleaved friction and drive plates, dramatically increasing torque capacity in the same diameter. Motorcycles and high-performance cars use wet multi-plate clutches running in oil, which improves cooling and longevity but reduces friction coefficient.

Diaphragm Spring Clutch

Modern vehicles use a diaphragm spring clutch where the coil spring assembly is replaced by a conical diaphragm (Belleville) spring. This provides a more progressive pedal feel, consistent clamp load across the wear range, and lower release force. The diaphragm acts as both the pressure plate spring and the release lever, reducing part count.

Uniform Pressure vs Uniform Wear Assumptions

Two design assumptions govern friction clutch calculations. The uniform pressure theory assumes constant contact pressure across the disc and gives Rm = (2/3) × (Ro³ − Ri³) / (Ro² − Ri²). The uniform wear theory assumes that wear is uniform and gives Rm = (Ro + Ri) / 2. Uniform wear is more realistic for a worn-in clutch; uniform pressure applies to a new clutch.
