📖 Quantum Physics Engine & Analytical Formulations

Classical electromagnetic wave theory failed to explain why light intensity could not cause electron emission below a threshold frequency and why emission occurred without any time lag. Albert Einstein (1905) resolved this by introducing light quantization ($E = h\nu$), earning the 1921 Nobel Prize in Physics.

1. Einstein's Photoelectric Equation:
Energy of incident photon: $$E_{\text{photon}} = h\nu = \frac{hc}{\lambda}$$ Maximum Kinetic Energy of emitted photoelectrons: $$E_k^{\text{max}} = h\nu - \Phi = h(\nu - \nu_0) = \frac{1}{2} m_e v_{\text{max}}^2$$ where $h = 6.626 \times 10^{-34}\text{ J}\cdot\text{s}$, $\Phi$ is cathode work function, and $\nu_0 = \Phi / h$ is threshold frequency.
2. Stopping Potential Formulation & Planck's Constant Extraction:
At stopping potential $V_0$, even electrons with maximum kinetic energy are brought to rest before reaching the collector anode: $$e V_0 = E_k^{\text{max}} = h\nu - \Phi \implies \mathbf{V_0 = \left(\frac{h}{e}\right)\nu - \frac{\Phi}{e}}$$ Plotting $V_0$ vs. $\nu$ yields a straight line with: $$\text{Slope } m = \frac{h}{e} \implies \mathbf{h = m \cdot e}$$ $$\text{Y-Intercept} = -\frac{\Phi}{e} \implies \mathbf{\Phi = e \cdot |\text{Y-Intercept}|}$$ $$\text{X-Intercept} = \nu_0 = \text{Threshold Frequency}$$
3. Photometry & Inverse Square Law ($I_{\text{sat}} \propto \frac{1}{d^2}$):
Radiation flux incident upon cathode surface from a point source at distance $d$: $$E_v = \frac{L_0}{d^2} \implies \text{Photon arrival rate } \dot{N}_\gamma \propto \frac{1}{d^2}$$ Because each absorbed photon ejects at most one photoelectron with quantum efficiency $\eta_Q$: $$\mathbf{I_{\text{sat}} \propto \frac{1}{d^2} \iff I_{\text{sat}} \cdot d^2 = \text{Constant}}$$

📚 Experimental Manual & Standard Operating Procedure

1. Aim of the Experiment & Apparatus

Aim: (a) To study the variation of photocurrent with retarding/accelerating potential for different wavelengths of light and extract Planck's constant ($h$) and work function ($\Phi$). (b) To verify the Inverse Square Law between photocurrent and source distance ($I_{\text{sat}} \propto 1/d^2$).

Apparatus: Evacuated photocell with quartz window, high-pressure halogen/mercury arc lamp, precision graduated optical rail (150 cm), optical bandpass filter set (365, 405, 436, 546, 578, 633 nm), DC regulated bias power supply, polarity reversing commutator, high-sensitivity digital electrometer ($\pm 0.01\text{ nA}$).

2. Step-by-Step Procedure: Planck's Constant Determination
  1. Select Cesium (Cs, $\Phi = 2.14\text{ eV}$) and position the light source at a fixed distance $d = 30.0\text{ cm}$.
  2. Insert the 405 nm (Violet) filter. Set bias voltage to $+1.0\text{ V}$ to confirm emission saturation ($I_{\text{sat}} \approx 5.8\text{ nA}$).
  3. Switch bias to reverse retarding mode. Gradually increase negative bias magnitude using the fine stepper buttons.
  4. Observe photoelectrons decelerating in the vacuum chamber. Locate the exact potential where photocurrent drops to $0.00\text{ nA}$. Record this as stopping potential $V_0$.
  5. Repeat steps 2–4 for 365 nm, 436 nm, 546 nm, and 578 nm filters.
  6. Log all points into Table A and verify the linear fit $V_0$ vs. $\nu$ to extract Planck's constant $h = m \cdot e$.
3. Step-by-Step Procedure: Inverse Square Law Verification
  1. Switch to Mode 2 (Inverse Square Law). Keep the Blue (436 nm) filter fixed and set bias to $+2.0\text{ V}$ (ensuring full saturation).
  2. Position the lamp carriage at $d = 20.0\text{ cm}$. Record $I_{\text{sat}}$ on the digital electrometer.
  3. Move the lamp carriage along the optical rail to $d = 30, 40, 50, 60, 80, 100\text{ cm}$. At each distance, log $I_{\text{sat}}$.
  4. Compute $1/d^2$ and the constancy product $I_{\text{sat}} \cdot d^2$. Verify that the product remains constant within $\pm 1\%$ experimental error.
4. Precautionary Guidelines & Experimental Sources of Error
  • Stray Ambient Light: Avoid direct room illumination falling into the photocell aperture to eliminate background dark current.
  • Thermal Drift: Avoid running the lamp at full intensity for excessively long periods without ventilation.
  • Contact Potential Difference: The work function difference between the cathode and anode materials induces a small contact potential ($\approx 0.05\text{ V}$), which is accounted for in high-precision laboratory electrometer setups.
